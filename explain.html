Spiral FFT: controls

Source code: github.com/adamfinkelstein/spiral

The left panel shows a white spiral (or set of concentric rings) on black, computed on a 512×512 grid. The right panel shows the 2D Fourier transform of that image: the log of its magnitude, with zero frequency at the center. Both panels show only their right two-thirds, since the images are nearly symmetric; the full image is still drawn and transformed. The blue lines mark the center of each canvas.

The Defaults button resets every control to its starting value and moves the center back to the middle.

Moving the center

Drag in the left image
Moves the center of the spiral (the red dot). The drag is relative to where you click, so you can grab anywhere. Hold Shift while dragging for 10× finer movement.
Arrow keys
Nudge the center by 1 pixel, or 0.1 pixel with Shift.
Double-click
Returns the center to the middle of the canvas.

Distances are in pixels of the 512×512 image. The center can sit at a fraction of a pixel, and the spiral is drawn exactly there.

Shape

Ring spacing (px)
Distance between successive turns of the spiral, or between rings. The spiral follows r = bθ with b = spacing / 2π, so the radius grows by one spacing per turn.
Start radius (px)
Radius where the spiral begins. In rings mode, the smallest ring drawn has at least this radius.
End radius (px)
Radius where the spiral ends. In rings mode, the largest ring drawn has at most this radius.
Rotation (deg)
Rotates the spiral about its center. Has no effect (and is greyed out) in rings mode.
Aspect (x, 1/y)
Scales the whole drawing about its center by the aspect value horizontally (0.95 to 1.05; 1 = no change) and by 1 / aspect vertically, so its area stays the same. Line widths scale too, as if the finished image had been resized. Ring spacing becomes spacing × aspect horizontally and spacing / aspect vertically, so the rings in the Fourier transform become slightly elliptical and the peaks in the radial profile smear. Use it to test how well the spacing estimate holds up when the drawing isn't perfectly circular.
Line width (px)
Width of the line, measured where it is at half brightness.
Rings/spiral
Checked: a spiral. Unchecked: concentric circles at radii that are multiples of the ring spacing, kept only if they fall between the start and end radii.

Line edges

Smooth line edges
Checked: each line edge fades smoothly from white to black, following a Gaussian blur (erf) profile. Unchecked: every pixel is either fully on or fully off, depending on whether its center falls inside a line.
Edge transition (px)
How wide the fade is, from 10% to 90% brightness. Only used with smooth edges.
Blur sigma (px)
Blurs the finished image with a Gaussian of this standard deviation before the Fourier transform (0 = no blur). The left panel shows the blurred image. Unlike smooth edges, which keep sharp detail from being drawn in the first place, blurring afterwards cannot undo aliasing that is already in the pixels: it dims the whole outer part of the spectrum, real content and aliased arcs alike.

Why this matters: sharp edges contain frequencies higher than the 512 grid can represent. In the Fourier transform those wrap around and show up as curved arcs coming in from the edges and corners of the right panel (aliasing). A smooth transition of about 2–3 px removes them.

Fourier transform

FFT contrast
Brightness curve for the right panel. Values above 1 brighten faint detail; values below 1 emphasize only the strongest parts. The display is scaled to the brightest value other than the zero-frequency point.
Subtract mean
Subtracts the image's average brightness before transforming, which removes the very bright spot at the center of the spectrum.
Hann window
Fades the image smoothly to black toward its borders before transforming. This suppresses the cross-shaped streaks that appear when the image has content near its edges, because the transform treats the image as repeating.
Show 1/spacing
Draws red circles on the spectrum at radius 512 / spacing and its multiples. These are where the energy from evenly spaced rings should land, so you can check the transform against them.

Radial profile plot

The plot below the two panels turns the 2D Fourier transform into two curves, both plotted against radius ρ: the distance from zero frequency in the right panel, measured in FFT bins (pixels of the 512×512 transform).

Where the peaks come from

A point at radius ρ in the transform is a pattern that repeats ρ times across the 512-pixel image, so its period is 512 / ρ pixels. Rings spaced s pixels apart therefore put their main bright ring at

ρ1 = 512 / s

Because each line is a narrow bright band rather than a smooth wave, the transform also has fainter rings at the multiples (harmonics) kρ1, k = 2, 3, … With spacing 10, for example, they are at 51.2, 102.4, 153.6, … With Show 1/spacing on, red dashed lines mark these expected positions.

White curve: radial profile

Let L(u,v) be the brightness of the right panel at contrast 1: the log magnitude log(1 + |F|), divided by its largest value away from zero frequency so it runs from 0 to 1. Each pixel is assigned to the bin given by its distance from the center, rounded to a whole number, and the white curve is the mean of each bin:

P(r) = (1 / nr) ∑ L(u,v)   over all pixels with round(√(u2 + v2)) = r

where nr is the number of pixels in bin r (roughly 2πr). Bright rings show up as peaks at kρ1. Beyond radius 256 (the shaded part) the circles only pass through the corners of the transform, so those bins hold fewer pixels. The contrast slider does not affect the plot.

Red curve: harmonic-sum score

The white curve's repeated peaks are turned into a single peak. For each candidate first-peak radius ρ, the score checks every multiple kρ and measures how far the profile there rises above the points halfway to the neighbouring multiples:

S(ρ) = (1 / √K) ∑k=1..K [ P̃(kρ) − ½ P̃((k−½)ρ) − ½ P̃((k+½)ρ) ]

Here P̃ is the smoothed profile (see below), read between bins by linear interpolation, and K is the number of multiples that fit: (K + ½)ρ ≤ 256.

  • Only the true ρ1 scores high. At ρ = ρ1 every term is a peak minus two troughs, so all terms are positive. At ρ1/2, every other term samples a trough. At 2ρ1, the halfway points land on peaks. Both give lower scores.
  • Subtracting the halfway points removes the slowly varying background of the profile, which falls steadily with radius.
  • Dividing by √K is a compromise found by testing. Dividing by K (an average) let a multiple of ρ1 that hits only one or two strong harmonics win. Not dividing at all let very small ρ win by adding up many small ripples.

The red curve is plotted at x = ρ, scaled to fill the plot height. Candidates cover ρ from 8 to about 171, i.e. spacings from 64 down to about 3 px.

From the peak to the spacing

The score is computed for ρ in steps of Δ = 0.05 and the highest sample Si is found. A parabola is then fit through it and its two neighbours to place the peak between samples:

ρ* = ρi + Δ · ½(Si−1 − Si+1) / (Si−1 − 2Si + Si+1)

and the estimated spacing is

s* = 512 / ρ*

The text in the plot's corner reports both numbers, and s* should match the ring spacing slider. The purple bar along the top of the plot shows s* graphically: it extends from the left edge to s* on its own scale, 0 to 64 px, labelled in purple across the top. Because ρ* uses every harmonic, not just the first peak, it is much more precise than reading one peak off the plot. In tests over spirals and rings, spacings 6–60 and small center offsets, the estimate was typically within 0.2–0.25% of the true spacing, and always within 0.5%.

Smoothing before scoring

Before the score is computed, the profile is blurred with a 1D Gaussian of standard deviation 1.5 bins, giving P̃. The white curve on the plot is still the unblurred P. The raw profile has small ripples: speckle, and fringes around each peak caused by the spiral starting and stopping at fixed radii (about 512 / (end − start radius) bins apart). These mostly corrupt the trough samples, where the background is low, and make the estimate jitter as parameters change. The blur removes them while leaving the peaks in place. In testing it cut the rms error for rings about 3× (0.65% to 0.21%) and slightly improved spirals. Blurring much more does not help: at 3 bins, the peaks for large spacings (only about 8 bins apart at spacing 60) merge, and the estimate fails.
